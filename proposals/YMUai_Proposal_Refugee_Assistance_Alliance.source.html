What Each of These Actually Is

In plain terms

Some of these terms are new to most organizations. Here is what they mean for your team.

AI use policy & values-aligned guardrails

A short, readable document telling your staff and volunteers what they can and cannot do with AI — which tools are approved, what client information never goes into one, and what to do when someone is unsure. Your volunteer model means people outside your payroll touch client information, which raises the stakes. It also gives you something concrete to point to when a board member or a funder asks how you are handling this.

Salesforce platform diagnostic

Salesforce runs an older nonprofit setup and a newer one. Which you are on determines which AI features are available to you at all. This is a one-time check that prevents you from paying for something your system cannot support.

Custom skills & AI assistants

A skill is a set of instructions built once and reused by everyone, so the tool handles a task the same way every time no matter who asks. An assistant is a larger version of the same idea, built around a specific workflow and trained on your own documents.

For your team that might mean turning intake notes into a standard client summary across all five programs, compiling English class enrollment and employment outcomes into the format your impact report needs, or drafting a funder progress report from your program data. Right now, if four people ask for the same thing four different ways, they get four different answers.

Contextual AI literacy training

Live working sessions, not lectures. Your team brings actual work, learns where these tools are strong and where they fail, and leaves with habits for verifying output. This is where the policy stops being a document and becomes practice.

Systems map & integration design

You mentioned three systems, and they do not all connect the same way. Your online giving platform is the easy one — platforms in that category typically build and maintain their own Salesforce connector, it works with either version of Salesforce for Nonprofits, and the cost is usually modest. If that connection is not switched on today, turning it on is a setup task rather than a project, and I will tell you so plainly rather than charge you to find out.

Your donor management system is a different situation. The platforms built specifically for small nonprofits are generally positioned as alternatives to Salesforce rather than partners to it, which means most do not publish a native Salesforce integration at all. Where that holds, connecting the two means building something custom — real work with real cost. That raises a question worth settling before anyone builds anything: whether two systems should both hold constituent records, or whether one becomes your source of truth. Getting it wrong is expensive in either direction.
